## 10-Mark Questions

---

### Q1. Define technical writing. Explain its nature, scope, importance, and characteristics.

**Definition:**
Technical writing is a form of writing that communicates specific information about a technical subject to a targeted audience. Its main goal is to make complex information easy to understand. Examples include user manuals, lab reports, project documentation, and research papers.

**Nature of Technical Writing:**
- It is **factual and objective** — no personal opinions or emotions.
- It is **audience-specific** — written for a particular group (engineers, doctors, students, etc.).
- It uses **simple, clear, and direct** language.
- It follows a **structured format** with headings, subheadings, lists, and diagrams.

**Scope of Technical Writing:**
Technical writing is used in many fields:
- **Engineering** — project reports, manuals, specifications
- **Medicine** — clinical reports, research papers
- **IT/Software** — user guides, API documentation, release notes
- **Science** — lab reports, thesis, journal articles
- **Business** — proposals, white papers, feasibility reports

**Importance of Technical Writing:**
1. Helps in **clear communication** of technical ideas.
2. Serves as **permanent record** of work done (reports, documentation).
3. Helps in **decision-making** — managers use reports to make decisions.
4. Required for **academic progress** — thesis, dissertations, research papers.
5. Improves **professional credibility** — well-written documents build trust.

**Characteristics of Technical Writing:**
| Characteristic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Clarity | Easy to understand, no ambiguity |
| Conciseness | Short and to the point, no unnecessary words |
| Accuracy | Facts and data must be correct |
| Objectivity | No personal bias or emotions |
| Accessibility | Organized with headings, lists, visuals |
| Audience-focused | Written keeping the reader in mind |
| Use of visuals | Tables, graphs, diagrams are commonly used |

### Q2. Explain various forms of scientific writing: thesis, dissertation, technical paper, manual, project report, and review article.

**1. Thesis:**
- A thesis is a long research document written by a student for a **master's degree**.
- It presents the student's own research, findings, and conclusions on a specific topic.
- Structure: Title → Abstract → Introduction → Literature Review → Methodology → Results → Discussion → Conclusion → References.

**2. Dissertation:**
- A dissertation is written for a **doctoral (PhD) degree**.
- It is longer and more detailed than a thesis.
- It must contain **original contribution** to the field of study.
- The student is expected to add new knowledge, not just study existing work.

**3. Technical Paper:**
- A short, focused document presenting research findings or a technical solution.
- Published in **journals or conference proceedings**.
- Structure: Title → Abstract → Introduction → Methodology → Results → Conclusion → References.
- It is peer-reviewed before publication.

**4. Manual:**
- A manual is a **step-by-step guide** that explains how to use a product, system, or software.
- Types: User manual, instruction manual, training manual, maintenance manual.
- It uses **simple language, numbered steps, diagrams, and screenshots**.

**5. Project Report:**
- A document that records the **entire process** of a project — from planning to completion.
- Common in engineering education and industry.
- Structure: Title page → Certificate → Acknowledgement → Abstract → Table of Contents → Introduction → Literature Review → Methodology → Implementation → Results → Conclusion → References → Appendix.

**6. Review Article:**
- A review article **summarizes and analyzes** existing research on a topic.
- It does **not** present new experimental data.
- It helps readers understand what research has been done and what gaps remain.
- Very useful for students beginning research in a new area.

| Form | Purpose | Length | Original Research? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thesis | Master's degree | Long | Yes |
| Dissertation | PhD degree | Very long | Yes (must be original) |
| Technical Paper | Share findings | Short (5–20 pages) | Yes |
| Manual | Guide the user | Varies | No |
| Project Report | Document a project | Medium | Sometimes |
| Review Article | Summarize existing research | Medium | No |

### Q3. Explain the components of a thesis and the role of literature review, results, discussion, and conclusion.

**Components of a Thesis (in order):**

1. **Title Page** — Title of the thesis, author name, university name, date.
2. **Abstract** — A short summary (150–300 words) of the entire thesis.
3. **Acknowledgement** — Thanks to guides, family, institution.
4. **Table of Contents** — List of all chapters and page numbers.
5. **List of Figures/Tables** — If applicable.
6. **Introduction** — Background of the topic, problem statement, objectives, scope.
7. **Literature Review** — Summary of existing research related to the topic.
8. **Methodology** — How the research was conducted (tools, methods, data collection).
9. **Results** — What was found (data, graphs, tables).
10. **Discussion** — What the results mean, comparison with other studies.
11. **Conclusion** — Final summary, contributions, future scope.
12. **References** — List of all sources cited.
13. **Appendix** — Extra material (code, questionnaires, raw data).

**Role of Key Components:**

**Literature Review:**
- Shows what research has already been done on the topic.
- Identifies the **research gap** — what has not been studied yet.
- Justifies why your research is needed.
- Demonstrates the researcher's understanding of the field.

**Results:**
- Presents the **data and findings** obtained from experiments or surveys.
- Uses tables, graphs, charts, and figures.
- Reports facts **without interpretation** — just "what happened."

**Discussion:**
- **Interprets the results** — explains what the data means.
- Compares findings with previous studies.
- Explains unexpected results.
- Discusses the **strengths and limitations** of the study.

**Conclusion:**
- Summarizes the **main findings** of the research.
- States whether the **objectives were achieved**.
- Highlights the **contribution** of the research.
- Suggests **future scope** — what can be studied next.

| Component | Key Role |
|---|---|
| Literature Review | Shows existing work + finds research gap |
| Results | Presents raw data and findings |
| Discussion | Interprets results + compares with others |
| Conclusion | Summarizes + states contribution + future work |

## 5-Mark Questions

---

### Q1. Technical writing vs Creative writing

| Feature | Technical Writing | Creative Writing |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | To inform or instruct | To entertain or express |
| Language | Simple, clear, formal | Figurative, expressive, emotional |
| Audience | Specific (engineers, users, scientists) | General public |
| Structure | Highly structured (headings, lists) | Flexible (stories, poems) |
| Tone | Objective, neutral | Subjective, personal |
| Examples | Manuals, reports, research papers | Novels, poems, short stories |
| Use of visuals | Tables, diagrams, charts | Rarely used |

**In short:** Technical writing focuses on **facts and clarity**, while creative writing focuses on **imagination and expression**.

---

### Q2. Abstract vs Introduction

| Feature | Abstract | Introduction |
|---|---|---|
| Position | Before the main text | First chapter/section of the document |
| Length | Short (150–300 words) | Longer (1–3 pages) |
| Purpose | Quick summary of the entire document | Provides background, context, and objectives |
| Content | Covers objective, method, results, conclusion | Covers background, problem, scope, objectives |
| Standalone? | Yes — can be read independently | No — leads into the rest of the document |
| When written? | Usually written last | Written first or early |

**In short:** Abstract is a **mini version** of the whole paper. Introduction is a **detailed setup** that prepares the reader for what's coming.

### Q3. Literature review and research gap

**Literature Review:**
A literature review is a **critical summary** of existing research on your topic. It includes:
- What studies have been done
- What methods were used
- What results were found
- How your topic fits into the bigger picture

**Research Gap:**
A research gap is something that **has not been studied yet** or an area where existing studies are incomplete. Identifying a research gap is important because:
- It **justifies** your research — shows why your study is needed.
- It **guides** your research objectives and methodology.

**Example:**
> "Many studies have explored the use of AI in medical diagnosis, but very few have focused on rural healthcare settings. This gap motivates the current study."

---

### Q4. Results vs Discussion vs Conclusion

| Feature | Results | Discussion | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it does | Presents raw data and findings | Interprets and explains the data | Summarizes the entire work |
| Interpretation? | No — just reports facts | Yes — explains what results mean | Brief summary of key findings |
| Comparison? | No | Yes — compares with other studies | No |
| Future scope? | No | Sometimes | Yes |
| Visuals? | Tables, charts, graphs | May reference visuals | Rarely |

**In short:** Results = **"What happened?"**, Discussion = **"What does it mean?"**, Conclusion = **"What's the final takeaway?"**

### Q5. Features of a project report

A project report is a document that records the complete work done during a project. Key features include:

1. **Structured format** — follows a standard layout (title page, abstract, chapters, references).
2. **Clear objectives** — states what the project aims to achieve.
3. **Detailed methodology** — explains how the project was carried out.
4. **Results and analysis** — presents the outcomes with supporting data.
5. **Diagrams and visuals** — uses flowcharts, screenshots, tables.
6. **Formal language** — written in third person, objective tone.
7. **References** — all sources are properly cited.
8. **Appendices** — extra material like code, data sheets attached at the end.

### Q6. Common abbreviations: i.e., e.g., et al., etc.

| Abbreviation | Full Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| i.e. | *id est* (Latin) | "that is" — used to clarify | The project uses OOP, i.e., Object-Oriented Programming. |
| e.g. | *exempli gratia* (Latin) | "for example" — used to give examples | Use a programming language, e.g., Python or Java. |
| et al. | *et alia* (Latin) | "and others" — used in citations with 3+ authors | (Sharma et al., 2023) |
| etc. | *et cetera* (Latin) | "and so on" — used to indicate more items exist | Include tables, charts, graphs, etc. |

**Tips:**
- Don't use "e.g." and "etc." together — they are redundant.
- "i.e." clarifies; "e.g." gives examples — they are **not** interchangeable.

### Q7. Formal email format

A formal email has the following parts:

1. **To** — Recipient's email address
2. **Subject line** — Clear and specific (e.g., "Request for Project Extension")
3. **Salutation** — "Dear Sir/Madam," or "Dear Prof. Sharma,"
4. **Body** —
   - Opening: State the purpose ("I am writing to request...")
   - Middle: Provide details
   - Closing: Polite ending ("I would be grateful if...")
5. **Closing line** — "Yours sincerely," or "Regards,"
6. **Signature** — Name, roll number, department, contact

**Example:**

> **To:** hod.cs@university.edu  
> **Subject:** Request for Project Deadline Extension  
>  
> Dear Sir/Madam,  
>  
> I am writing to request an extension of the project submission deadline. Due to some technical issues with the server, our team could not complete the testing phase on time.  
>  
> I kindly request an extension of one week. We will ensure timely submission.  
>  
> Yours sincerely,  
> Rahul Sharma  
> Roll No: 21CS045  
> Department of Computer Science
